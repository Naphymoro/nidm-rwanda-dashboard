Full Stress-Test Walkthrough

The stress test should use the real workflow. Do not preload narratives. Start from Stage 1, upload one corpus file, check that the repository ledger is created, then move through governance, encoding, modelling, learning, synthesis, inoculation, and policy output.

1. Narrative ingestion

Click or select

Choose the evidence route, then click Open text or CSV file in Step 2 and select the matching file from stress_test_corpus/. Use Load one sample story only for a quick single-story manual intake test.

Expect to see

Country, administrative units, language, source, period, route fields, and narrative text should populate or remain editable.

Interpretation

The tool is turning a field source into a structured observation without deciding yet whether the evidence is trustworthy.

Policy implication

Good policy starts with traceable provenance: who said what, where, when, and under which consent conditions.

Watch for

Wrong route, missing district/sector, blank source name, or a CSV that collapses many narratives into one text block.

2. SDMX gate and governance review

Click or select

Check Step 6 readiness immediately below the narrative body, then click Stage and validate. In the SDMX gate, review visibility, consent, repository target, and risk threshold.

Expect to see

Governed records, content hashes, scan status, approval queue, and a contract table showing required fields.

Interpretation

The gate is an evidence firewall. It separates raw narrative collection from model-ready observations.

Policy implication

Records with weak consent, poor provenance, or high injection risk should not drive official policy claims.

Watch for

Flagged records, missing hashes, private records being treated as public, or source fields that are too vague to audit later.

3. Narrative repository / evidence ledger

Click or select

After staging, go to the SDMX gate. In the approval queue, click Approve or Reject. Then click Commit reviewed records. Use Open full repository to inspect active review, accepted, and rejected sections.

Expect to see

Before commit, reviewed records stay in the active approval queue as pending commit. After commit, approved records disappear from the queue and appear in the accepted repository; rejected records appear in the rejected repository. Accepted or rejected rows have an Uncommit button.

Interpretation

The active review queue is a review workspace. The accepted repository is the model-ready evidence set. The rejected repository is an audit-preserved exclusion set. Uncommit is a correction path when a decision needs to be reopened.

Policy implication

Only accepted repository records should influence encoding, modelling, and policy output. Rejected records still matter for audit because they explain what evidence was excluded and why.

Watch for

Approving but forgetting to commit, accepted records without seals, rejected records being encoded, duplicated rows after batch upload, or route labels that do not match the selected corpus file.

4. Encoding options: manual, AI, hybrid, and comparison

Click or select

Move to Encoding. Select manual, AI, or hybrid. For batches, use story-by-story controls and compare all modes when testing scoring consistency.

Expect to see

Scores for trust, barrier, confidence, adoption stance, and narrative Phi inputs, with justifications and comparison blocks when multiple modes run.

Interpretation

Encoding converts language into model inputs. Manual scores reveal researcher judgement; AI scores reveal machine interpretation; hybrid checks disagreement.

Policy implication

Large disagreement between encoders means the evidence should be reviewed before it affects model dynamics.

Watch for

Unsupported LLM provider, missing user key, skipped score explanations, or batch imports that encode only the first story.

5. Compartmental model

Click or select

Open Model, then run the compartmental model. Review the equations and the S, M, T, I, R population curves.

Expect to see

Aggregate diffusion dynamics: susceptible households, misinformation pressure, trusted adoption, informed adoption, and resistant/inoculated groups.

Interpretation

This model asks how narratives change adoption at population scale. Phi should change transition rates, not replace evidence.

Policy implication

If trusted adoption rises slowly, outreach or trust-building may matter more than equipment availability alone.

Watch for

Running the model before approved evidence exists, over-reading synthetic data, or treating aggregate curves as district-level truth.

6. Agent-based model

Click or select

Run the agent model after the compartmental model. Review household trust, peer influence, outreach, barriers, and network effects.

Expect to see

Local adoption variation and household-level friction that may not appear in aggregate curves.

Interpretation

The agent model explains why two places with similar evidence may respond differently because households influence each other through networks.

Policy implication

Peer demonstrations, trusted messengers, repair access, and women-group networks may shift adoption more than broad messaging.

Watch for

Ignoring low-trust clusters, assuming all households receive information equally, or using agent output without comparing it to the ODE output.

7. Digital twin feedback

Click or select

Run the digital twin feedback stage. Enter or review field feedback such as adoption observations, trust shift, or barrier correction.

Expect to see

The model reruns with feedback-adjusted assumptions and reports how the adoption trajectory changes.

Interpretation

The twin is a learning loop: observations correct the model instead of letting the first simulation become fixed truth.

Policy implication

If feedback lowers adoption expectations, policy should slow down claims, target barriers, or request more evidence.

Watch for

Feedback entered without provenance, feedback that contradicts the ledger, or using the twin as an automatic decision maker.

8. Bayesian update

Click or select

Run Bayesian update after the twin. Review priors, observed evidence, and posterior trust or barrier assumptions.

Expect to see

Prior-to-posterior movement showing how evidence changes uncertainty around trust, barriers, or adoption probability.

Interpretation

Bayesian updating says how much the model should learn from evidence while preserving uncertainty.

Policy implication

Wide uncertainty should produce cautious recommendations, pilot designs, or more data collection rather than a final national claim.

Watch for

Priors that are too confident, posteriors that move without evidence, or treating synthetic corpus results as real posterior knowledge.

9. RL optimizer

Click or select

Run the RL optimizer. Review intervention options, episode rewards, learned policy, and best adoption fraction.

Expect to see

A learning curve and recommended intervention package, usually balancing outreach, subsidy, trust messaging, and support.

Interpretation

RL tests which action packages improve the simulated objective. It is a scenario search tool, not a moral or political authority.

Policy implication

Use RL output to shortlist policies for human review, budget feasibility, equity review, and district consultation.

Watch for

High reward with unrealistic cost, recommendations that ignore governance status, or overfitting to synthetic stress-test narratives.

10. Regional analysis

Click or select

Run regional analysis. Isolate one district or group places to compare narrative signals across locations.

Expect to see

Place-specific differences in barriers, trust, adoption stance, and evidence strength.

Interpretation

Regional analysis asks whether a national intervention should be adapted to local narrative conditions.

Policy implication

A district with safety fear needs different action from a district with cost pressure or repair-access distrust.

Watch for

Small sample sizes, mixing provinces without reason, or ignoring that synthetic records are only test cases.

11. Knowledge graph

Click or select

Build the knowledge graph after records are encoded. Inspect links among stories, places, themes, barriers, motivators, and trusted messengers.

Expect to see

Clusters showing repeated themes from the same location or across locations, such as cost, safety, smoke, time savings, repair trust, or status.

Interpretation

The graph is a qualitative sense-making layer. It shows which ideas travel together and which locations share narrative structure.

Policy implication

If multiple districts connect safety fear with repair distrust, policy should include service guarantees, demonstrations, and trusted local technicians.

Watch for

Graph clusters based on unapproved records, duplicate stories creating false centrality, or ignoring cultural sensitivity fields.

12. Inoculation lab

Click or select

Generate inoculation narratives. Choose audience and tone, then review pre-bunking and refutation drafts. If ODE and agent models have run, click Apply narrative vaccine to digital twin.

Expect to see

Counter-narratives plus a model panel showing intervention strength and before, during, and after adoption curves for the compartmental and agent models.

Interpretation

Inoculation theory is at work when people receive a small preview of misinformation plus a clear refutation before the stronger rumor spreads. In the model, that narrative vaccine increases trust/resistance and reduces barrier pressure.

Policy implication

Messages should be locally grounded, evidence-traceable, and delivered through trusted messengers. A higher after-curve suggests the message is worth piloting, not that it is automatically proven.

Watch for

Counter-feeds that stigmatize communities, expose sensitive indigenous knowledge, make claims beyond approved evidence, or are exported without human review.

13. Policy output

Click or select

Run the policy output after synthesis. Review recommendation, evidence trail, uncertainty, governance status, model results, and human review notes.

Expect to see

A human-readable policy brief first, plus HTML/PDF-ready export controls and a separate JSON audit payload with records, hashes, modelling outputs, posterior learning, RL recommendation, inoculation status, and synthesis results.

Interpretation

The policy output should explain what the tool recommends and why, while showing the chain of evidence, uncertainty, and whether the inoculation vaccine changed the digital twin.

Policy implication

The strongest output is not just a recommendation. It is a defensible decision package that a researcher or policy maker can audit.

Watch for

Policy output without approved records, missing evidence seal, missing uncertainty language, or recommendations that ignore vulnerable groups.

14. Export and interpretation

Click or select

Use Download HTML brief or Open PDF view for human sharing. Use Copy/Download JSON only when you need the technical audit payload.

Expect to see

Export formats for a readable HTML policy brief, a print-to-PDF view, Markdown brief copy, and a structured JSON audit payload.

Interpretation

HTML/PDF is for decision meetings. JSON is for reproducibility and audit. The interactive screen is for review and rerunning stages.

Policy implication

Separate technical audit files from decision briefs so government users can share only what permissions allow.

Watch for

Sharing private records, exporting synthetic stress-test data as real evidence, or losing the link between recommendation and narrative IDs.

What To Look For

  • The upload path should be the same as real research data. There should be no special loader or shortcut.
  • Every imported record should carry location, source, language, period, consent, visibility, and hash metadata.
  • Structured interview data should not collapse Q1-Q5 into one undifferentiated block.
  • Batch imports should allow story-by-story encoding and comparison blocks when more than one encoding mode is used.
  • Social media feed records should support risk sensing and inoculation design without becoming automatic policy evidence.
  • Policy output should explain evidence strength, uncertainty, regional relevance, and implications.
